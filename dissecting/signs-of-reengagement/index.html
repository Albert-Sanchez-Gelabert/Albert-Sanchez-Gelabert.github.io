Elias, Masjuan & Sánchez-Gelabert · 2012

THE RESEARCH PROJECT BEHIND THE CHAPTER

This chapter grew out of Los estudiantes ante la nueva reforma universitaria (National R&D Plan, CSO2008-02812), a GRET project involving researchers from UAB and UB, with AQU Catalunya as a promoting/observing institution. The wider study examined the Bologna reforms and student experience across ten university programmes.

One published chapter, opened up. The interview excerpts shown here are taken from the published text; the research files help reconstruct the analytical decisions, not recreate a complete audit trail.

01 / 07THE QUESTION

When does participation become engagement?

McInnis (2002) described changing participation among Australian undergraduates. In Catalonia, teaching practices associated with the Bologna Process were reshaping the time and contact expected of students. Could those changes point towards reengagement?

The study does not replicate an Australian survey or provide a causal evaluation of Bologna. It asks how institutional practices and student experiences may reshape forms of participation and identification.

02 / 07PREVIOUS WORK

A dialogue, not a refutation.

A prior study helps define what is worth observing. The original literature on student engagement and university identity also shows why attendance, participation and a feeling of belonging should not be treated as synonyms.

McInnis provides the provocation; the literature on identity and integration provides distinctions with which to interpret the interviews.

03 / 07THE CONCEPTS

Being present is not belonging.

The study separates what students do from what they feel or value. It also distinguishes identification with the academic sphere from relationships and participation in the social life of the institution.

A student may be academically active but socially detached; socially connected but critical of teaching; or present mainly because attendance and assessment are compulsory.

04 / 07THE DATA

Why these 80 interviews?

The qualitative phase covered ten degree programmes across four public universities in metropolitan Barcelona: two programmes in each of five subject areas, selected to vary the clarity of their professional orientation.

Eight interviews per programme provided a basis for comparing accounts across contrasting disciplinary and institutional settings. This is purposive qualitative variation, not a representative national sample.

05 / 07THE MEASURE

How do words become evidence?

In this qualitative design, the issue is not to assign a single numerical score to engagement. It is to decide what questions might reveal its different dimensions—and which passages of an interview can illuminate them.

To analyse the interviews, the researchers developed a codebook: a document organising the topics to look for in students’ accounts, such as a sense of belonging, relationships with classmates and contact with lecturers. Codes are linked to questions in the interview guide, but can be used for any relevant passage in an interview.
A concrete example:1 · From the guide to the topics. Question 10 addresses relationships among classmates and with lecturers. The codebook contains categories for both themes.2 · From accounts to codes. We read the transcripts, select passages discussing these themes and tag them with the relevant codes.3 · From passages to interpretation. We compare accounts from different students, looking for similarities and differences. For example, more contact with classmates does not always mean a stronger sense of belonging to the university.
IN DETAIL
06 / 07THE METHODOLOGICAL STRATEGY

How does an excerpt become an argument?

An interview excerpt is evidence of what one participant says—not a conclusion by itself. The analysis relates it to a conceptual category, compares it with other accounts and asks whether a different case changes the interpretation.

The analysis notes bring together passages from different interviews to examine ideas about participation and identification with university. They also include accounts that qualify or challenge those ideas. Comparing them helps build an interpretation that goes beyond a single case.

07 / 07THE CONCLUSIONS

What can reengagement mean?

Some pedagogical changes generated more contact, friendship and opportunities to learn with others. But they also brought additional obligations, uneven experiences and distinctions between attending, identifying with a course and belonging to an institution.

The study suggests possible signs of reengagement—not an unconditional success story about Bologna or proof that teaching reform caused stronger institutional attachment.
