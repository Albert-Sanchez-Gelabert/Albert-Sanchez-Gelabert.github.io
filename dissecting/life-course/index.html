The study we are opening
Higher education and the life course: Exploring the interactive effects of age and employment status on university graduation

Albert Sánchez-Gelabert · 2024

READ THE ARTICLE ↗
STUDY 03 / EDITORIAL DRAFT

A guided reading of the published research process. Observed results are identified; methodological illustrations are not estimated data.

Non-traditional students · Working students · Mize (2019)

A research process, not an interaction-effect calculator.

01 / 07THE QUESTION

Does work mean the same at every age?

Working while studying and entering university later in life are often treated as separate challenges. But comparing which one “matters more” may miss a more revealing question: does the association between work and graduation change with age?

The research moves from ranking age and employment as separate predictors to asking whether the work–graduation association varies across the life course.

02 / 07PREVIOUS WORK

What does previous research tell us?

Research on non-traditional students connects persistence to educational pathways, employment and responsibilities outside university. Earlier Catalan findings helped sharpen the question, but they are one part of a wider debate.

Previous research offers competing expectations about work and age. The gap is not a lack of single-variable comparisons, but limited knowledge of how their association with graduation changes across ages.

03 / 07THE CONCEPTS

Why does life timing matter?

Age is more than a control variable. Within a life-course perspective, it marks different timings of educational entry, work, responsibilities and possible interruptions in earlier trajectories.

The same observed employment status can be embedded in different life circumstances; the study examines whether its association with graduation changes across those circumstances.

04 / 07THE DATA

Who can we observe?

The study starts with a cohort of first-time entrants to face-to-face public universities in Catalonia in 2012, then follows administrative graduation records for eight years.

Studying entrants rather than only graduates changes which questions are observable. It does not eliminate every form of selection or missing information.

05 / 07THE MEASURE

How do we define age, work and graduation?

There is no single scale to invent here. The crucial measurement decisions concern the age at which students enter, whether they work at that moment, and what counts as completing a university degree.

Operationalisation makes the research question testable, but it also defines which differences the study can and cannot detect.

06 / 07THE METHODOLOGICAL STRATEGY

Why model an interaction?

A binary graduation outcome motivates a logistic model. But a single coefficient for paid work cannot tell us whether its association with graduation is stable across age.

The analytical strategy therefore includes an age-by-work interaction and interprets differences in predicted probabilities, following the logic of Mize (2019).

07 / 07THE CONCLUSIONS

What can we conclude?

Working at entry is associated with a lower probability of graduation for much of the age range, but the difference is not uniform. The finding gains meaning when read through the research question, the cohort and the model.

The contribution is not a universal effect of working: it is evidence that looking only at average associations hides relevant life-course heterogeneity.
