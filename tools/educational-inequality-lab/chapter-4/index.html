01 / An expectation

Is academic success a straight line?

We often picture university as a predictable journey: enter, pass each year, graduate.

But academic progress is not a single event. Students advance, struggle, recover, slow down or stop. The order in which these things happen matters.

What changes if we follow students over time, instead of only looking at whether they graduate?

We followed the progression of 30,392 university entrants in Catalonia across seven academic years.

02 / The first discovery

There is more than one way to earn a degree.

The most common pattern is straightforward: consistently high performance. But it is not the only route that leads to graduation.

A strong start does not guarantee a straight ending.

One smaller group maintains relatively high academic performance for several years. Looking only at the beginning of their studies, we might expect them to finish.

What happens in the years after this apparently strong beginning?

Progress can disappear late in the journey.

The group's progress falls sharply after the initial years. By the end of the observation period, 67.1% of the students assigned to this trajectory had dropped out.

A good beginning and a good ending are not the same thing.

04 / The sociological meaning

Recovery takes time. And time is not equally available.

The same academic system does not offer the same practical room for extending or redirecting a university journey.

The study finds that older entrants are generally less likely to follow a recovery trajectory, particularly in demanding fields. Some follow an optimal route; others leave. Gradual recovery is less common.

Work commitments, family responsibilities, the costs of extra years and the academic demands of different fields are plausible reasons why extending a degree may be more feasible for some students than others. The study identifies patterned trajectories, not the direct causes of every decision.

ANOTHER WAY TIME MATTERS

In the slow-progression group, 74.2% were still enrolled after seven years. Persistence and graduation are not synonymous.

05 / Your sociological insight

A degree is an outcome. Progression is a journey.

The discovery

Some students improve and graduate later. Others start strongly but leave. The timing and sequence of academic experiences matter.

Looking only at who graduates hides the diversity of university experiences. And looking only at performance in the first year hides what happens next. Paths of recovery, persistence and departure are unevenly distributed across students' circumstances and fields of study.

Success is not one straight line. Understanding inequality means looking at the paths students can follow — and the time those paths require.
